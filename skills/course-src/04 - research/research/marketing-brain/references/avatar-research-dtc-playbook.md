# Internet Avatar Research DTC Playbook

Adapted from the uploaded avatar research playbook for this project. This version intentionally excludes interview recruiting, focus group operations, manual customer calls, and live survey fieldwork unless the user explicitly asks for them.

## Core Thesis

Good avatar research starts with the decision, not the data. Every research task should answer:

- What decision are we making?
- What evidence would change that decision?
- What do we already know?
- What internet sources can answer the unknowns fastest?
- What should we ignore because it will not change the creative, offer, or funnel?

For DTC, the output is not "customer profile." The output is a sharper ad angle, hook, landing-page objection stack, proof gap, offer improvement, or creative test matrix.

## Decision-First Research Map

Use this before scraping or reading sources:

1. State the business problem:
   - Example: "We need to decide which cold Meta angle deserves first spend."
   - Bad version: "Research the avatar."
2. List the options:
   - Example: deleted-photo shame, dental quote shock, cheap clip-on fear, wedding deadline.
3. Define what would change the decision:
   - repeated high-intensity phrases
   - buyer behavior, not only opinions
   - competitor review gaps
   - objections that appear near purchase
   - trigger events that appear across sources
4. Research only the low-knowledge items.
5. End with a recommended action.

## Evidence Buckets

Tag every useful insight into one of three buckets.

### Behavioral

What people actually do:

- bought, compared, searched, tried, refunded, quit, recommended, complained
- purchase recency or repeat behavior
- current workaround
- competitor usage
- category shopping behavior

Behavior validates market reality.

### Attitudinal

What people think and feel:

- fear, shame, resentment, skepticism, desire, hope, confusion
- objections and beliefs
- dream outcome
- language they use with peers

Attitude creates hooks and copy.

### Classification

Who or what situation they are in:

- life stage
- trigger event
- problem duration
- category experience
- purchase stage
- decision-maker vs. user vs. payer

Classification matters only when it changes the ad, offer, or proof required.

## Internet Source Screen

Prioritize sources that show at least one:

- recent buyer or search behavior
- category comparison
- failed alternative
- objection or refund language
- emotionally specific pain
- trigger event that caused active search
- dream outcome
- competitor praise or complaint

Weak sources:

- generic comments with no lived context
- low-engagement opinions
- brand-written copy
- vague "this looks cool" reactions
- content with no purchase or problem signal

## Best Internet Sources

Use according to product:

- Owned reviews: proof language, outcomes, objections, customer support themes.
- Competitor reviews: market gaps, unmet expectations, positioning weaknesses.
- 1-star reviews: friction, refund fear, product limitations, broken promises.
- 5-star reviews: trigger, skepticism, belief shift, outcome, surprise benefit.
- Reddit/forums: authentic problem language, failed alternatives, taboo emotions.
- TikTok/YouTube comments: fast emotional reactions, objections, identity signals.
- Meta Ad Library: competitor hypotheses and repeated spend signals.
- Landing pages and FAQs: objection maps and proof priorities.
- Search autosuggest/PAA: active search language and question framing.

## Trigger Event Hunt

The highest-value avatar output is the moment someone goes from passive sufferer to active searcher.

Ask of every source:

- What happened right before they started looking?
- What made the problem impossible to ignore?
- Was there a deadline, social event, embarrassment, diagnosis, breakup, photo, bill, quote, or failed attempt?
- What phrase describes that moment?

Trigger events become TOF hooks and targeting logic.

## Pain Ladder

Do not stop at surface pain. Ladder down:

1. Surface pain: the obvious problem.
2. Practical cost: what it costs in time, money, convenience, effort.
3. Emotional/social cost: shame, avoidance, frustration, judgment, fear.
4. Identity/status fear: what it makes them believe about themselves or how others see them.

Example for a home improvement product:

- Surface: "My house is always cold in one room."
- Practical: "My energy bill keeps climbing and I still need blankets."
- Emotional: "I feel stupid paying more and still being uncomfortable."
- Identity/status: "My home does not feel like it is under control."

The strongest cold ads usually start around levels 3-4, then bridge back to the product mechanism.

## Review Mining Process

For each owned and competitor review, extract:

- exact phrase
- star rating or sentiment
- trigger event
- prior failed alternative
- prior skepticism
- outcome
- surprise benefit
- objection or friction
- proof element
- ad-use note

Use 5-star reviews for:

- desired transformation
- proof language
- belief shift
- testimonial structure
- "I was skeptical but..." sequences

Use 1-star reviews for:

- buyer fears
- product limitations
- trust gaps
- competitor weaknesses
- landing-page objection sections
- retargeting angles

## Competitor Marketing As Hypotheses

Competitor ads and landing pages are not truth. They are spend-backed hypotheses.

Extract:

- lead pain
- promise
- mechanism
- proof
- price framing
- guarantee/risk reversal
- CTA
- FAQ objections
- testimonials featured
- repeated creative themes

Then decide:

- Copy: use a proven category convention.
- Counter-position: attack a weakness.
- Avoid: skip a saturated or low-belief angle.
- Escalate: take the same desire deeper or more specific.

## Frequency x Intensity Scoring

For each theme, score 1-5:

- Frequency: how often it appears across independent sources.
- Emotional intensity: how vivid or charged the language is.
- Buyer intent: how close the speaker seems to purchase.
- Source credibility: how real and relevant the source is.
- Creative usefulness: how easily it becomes a hook, visual, or LP section.
- Proof availability: whether the product can credibly support the promise.

Do not let frequency alone decide. A rare but nuclear phrase can beat a common bland complaint.

## Quote-Evidence-Implication Format

Present findings like this:

1. Theme: the market insight.
2. Evidence: exact phrases or summarized source evidence.
3. Implication: what to change in ads, landing page, offer, proof, or retargeting.

Bad:

"People want lower bills."

Good:

"The dominant TOF insight is not saving money in the abstract, it is frustration with paying more while still feeling uncomfortable at home. Evidence: repeated phrases around cold rooms, rising bills, blankets indoors, and avoiding one room in the house. Implication: lead cold ads with the daily irritation before introducing the product mechanism."

## Internet-Only Probe Toolkit

When reading comments, reviews, and threads, mentally probe:

- What are they really saying under the surface?
- What did they try before this?
- What would make them believe?
- What are they afraid will happen if they buy?
- What are they afraid will happen if they do nothing?
- What is the social cost?
- What phrase sounds like a real ad hook?
- Is this buyer, user, researcher, or payer speaking?

## Quantification Rules

Do not turn qualitative internet research into fake precision.

Allowed:

- "recurring theme"
- "high-intensity theme"
- "appears across Reddit and reviews"
- "strongest buyer-intent signal"
- "source count: 18 relevant comments reviewed, 7 mention fit anxiety"

Not allowed unless you actually calculated a sample:

- "62% of the market"
- "most customers"
- "everyone says"
- "statistically proven"

## Creative Translation

Every research run should produce:

- top 3 trigger event hooks
- top 3 pain ladders
- top 5 objections in priority order
- failed alternative map
- dream outcome language
- buyer archetypes by belief state
- quote bank by emotion
- landing-page objection sections
- cold TOF angle recommendations
- BOF proof and price angles
- retargeting angles to remove inhibition

## Operator Rules

- Research to decide, not to know.
- Behavior beats stated intent.
- Exact language beats paraphrase.
- Trigger events beat demographics.
- Pain depth beats surface problem labels.
- Segment only when the segment changes the ad.
- Competitor complaints are offer opportunities.
- A good research output ends with what to test next.
