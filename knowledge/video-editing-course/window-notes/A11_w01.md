## What is taught
The hook's visuals and text must work together to give the viewer instant context. Confusion ("what am I looking at?") loses people. Intrigue ("I need to know how this works") keeps them. Hook text is a separate job from lip-sync captions: it states why the viewer should care, in specific and relatable terms. It must be on screen from the very first frame, with no animated entrance.

## Concrete rules and numbers
- Every hook element, visual or text, should combine to give instant context. If the viewer thinks "what is this / what am I looking at / what's going on", the hook has lost them.
- Confusion and intrigue are different things:
  - Confusion: "I don't understand what I'm looking at." It loses attention.
  - Intrigue: "I need to know how this works." It challenges a belief so the viewer wants to learn more, and it captures attention.
  - Aim for intrigue.
  - Instructor's example of intrigue: "lost five kilograms while still eating pizza every other day."
- Hook text is not the lip-synced caption at the bottom of the video. It is a separate element with a specific job: telling people why they should care.
- Avoid generic claims in hook text. His example of a bad one is "this is the best eye protection ever", placed at the top of the screen, because nobody believes that anymore.
- Write specific, relatable, problem-solving text instead. His example: "I stopped getting headaches and still grind Minecraft every night for five hours." It targets a particular audience (gamers with eye strain who wake up with headaches) and gives the video context.
- Timing: hook text must be there from frame one.
  - No fancy animation.
  - No sliding in after two seconds.
  - No pop-in.
  - Reason: by the time the animation finishes, the viewer is "three videos down their feed" because the text was too hard to scan.
- The hook text in his example sits at the top of the screen.
