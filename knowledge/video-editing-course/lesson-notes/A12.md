# A12 — 12 Mr Beast Retention Techniques (Video Ads Mastery)

**One-line summary.** Mr Beast's retention psychology carries over to short ads. The lesson covers three techniques: stack open loops and hold the product reveal back, never let the viewer's eyes get comfortable (something changes every 1–3 seconds, paced to the audience), and use progress bars and countdown timers so viewers know how long they must keep watching.

Note: the title promises 12 techniques, but the recording covers only three, which the instructor recaps at the end.

## Key lessons

1. **Open a loop in the first seconds.** In the Mr Beast example, the hook "Will he survive 50 hours buried alive?" is the first loop. [00:00–03:00]
2. **Layer more loops on top of the first.** Each new problem or reveal must raise a new question the viewer needs closed, not just add information. Mr Beast's layers were a storm and a possible coffin flood, dropping oxygen levels, and the support team considering pulling him out. In ads, do the same thing "just faster". [00:00–03:00]
3. **Example four-loop stack for a weight-loss supplement ad.** [00:00–03:00]
   - Someone who lost 50 lb opens "how did they do it?"
   - The same person eats a box of pizza and still looks fit, which opens "they can eat that and look like that?"
   - A before/after photo where the "before" is only 90 days old opens "how did it change so fast?"
   - Testimonials of others getting the same results open "does it work for everyone?"
4. **The principle is length-independent.** It holds for 3 seconds, 3 minutes or 30 minutes. [00:00–03:00]
5. **Hold back the product reveal.** Showing the product early puts the viewer in "skeptic mode" ("just another scammy supplement"). Stack seemingly impossible results first, such as losing a lot of weight "while eating whatever they want in 90 days, and it works for everyone". The viewer moves from "no way" to "what if this is the one thing I've been missing?". By the reveal they are "waiting for the answer". [03:00–06:00]
6. **Don't front-load effort.** A fast hook followed by energy drops and weird pacing loses trust. Viewers notice when the first 3 seconds got all the effort, and they leave. Be "obsessive about every frame". [03:00–06:00]
7. **Visual variety (principle #1 of the editing section): "never let your eyes get comfortable".** Mr Beast's cadence is a new camera angle every 3–5 s, a new scene or development every 10 s, and a new mini-story beat every 20–30 s. The sentence about the last beat was cut off in the notes. [03:00–06:00]
8. **Every visual switch needs a purpose.** [06:00–09:00]
   - Close-ups for emotional moments.
   - Wide shots or big zoom-outs for scale and impact.
   - Reaction shots for authenticity, using raw or behind-the-scenes footage.
   - Deliberate speed-ups or slow-downs.
9. **Compress the cadence for ads.** Something must change every 2–3 seconds, and the instructor would "even say every one second". A change can be a B-roll change, a new animation, new text, a new angle or a new product shot. People scroll "at light speed", so fresh visual information every couple of seconds stops them leaving. [06:00–09:00]
10. **Match pacing to the audience's age and habits.** [06:00–09:00]
    - Gen Z: something every single second, with quick "brain rot" edits.
    - People in their sixties: fast, hectic editing is probably the worst choice because they can't keep track.
    - In general, make the style resemble the content that audience already consumes daily.
11. **Progress bar psychology (technique two).** In Mr Beast's sponsor segments the bar fills fast at first, about 50% in the first 10 s, so the viewer thinks "this will be quick". It then slows and the second half takes much longer, but by then the viewer is invested and finishes. [06:00–09:00]
12. **Use a visible timer in ads.** Don't jump straight to before/after. Talk about something else first, show a small timer such as "see results in 15 seconds", and start it. This sets a clear expectation of how long to pay attention. [~08:15]
13. **Testimonials get a progress bar.** Don't run them back to back with nothing between them. Add a small progress bar that fills with each testimonial. [09:00–12:00]
14. **VSL-style ads use a countdown before the reveal.** [09:00–12:00]
    - Spend the first part on the problem, pain points and struggles.
    - Mention the "breakthrough solution" now and then, without revealing it or showing the product.
    - Just before the reveal, show a title like "revolutionary solution revealed in 20 seconds" and start a countdown.
    - Keep reinforcing the earlier points while the timer runs, so viewers are on the edge of their seat when it hits zero.
15. **Why timers work.** People stay longer when they know exactly how much longer they must pay attention, and seeing progress rewards them as they watch. [09:00–12:00]
16. **Recap.** The instructor calls visual variety the most important of the three. A scroll-stopping hook only earns a pause, and retention principles keep people watching. [09:00–12:00]
    - Open loops: stack reveal after reveal, each more intriguing than the last.
    - Visual variety: never let the viewer's eyes get comfortable, and match editing pace to the audience.
    - Progress psychology: timers and progress bars, with some reward for completing parts of the video.

## Editing recipe

The lesson is mostly talk with illustrative overlays, not a step-by-step edit. The implied order for an ad is:
1. Write the script as a stack of loops. Each beat raises a new question, and the product stays hidden.
2. Open with the biggest impossible-seeming result (the first loop) in the first seconds.
3. Edit so something changes every 1–3 s: B-roll, animation, text, angle or product shot. Choose the cadence to suit the target age group.
4. Give each change a purpose: close-up for emotion, wide for scale, raw reaction for authenticity, speed ramps.
5. Hold energy through the whole ad, not only the hook.
6. Early in the ad, or before a reveal, add a timer ("see results in 15 seconds", "revolutionary solution revealed in 20 seconds") and run it down while reinforcing earlier points.
7. For testimonial runs, add a small progress bar that fills with each testimonial.
8. Reveal the product only when the timer hits zero, after the viewer is hopeful and waiting.

## How an ffmpeg/Python agent applies this

1. **Open the loop in the first seconds (lesson 1).** JUDGE. A model or human must check that the first seconds pose a clear question. For AI-generated scripts, add a rule that the first line states a question or an impossible result.
2. **Stack loops, each reveal raising a new question (lessons 2–3).** JUDGE for the script. Have an LLM tag each beat with the question it opens and flag beats that only add information. AUTOMATE the check as a prompt over the transcript or shot list.
3. **Length-independent principle (lesson 4).** SKIP as a separate action. It is a rationale, not a task.
4. **Hold the product reveal (lesson 5).** AUTOMATE a partial check. Transcribe with Whisper and flag the first mention or on-screen appearance of the product name. Compare that timestamp to the total length and warn if it appears early. JUDGE whether the viewer is "waiting" by then.
5. **Don't front-load effort (lesson 6).** AUTOMATE. Compute cuts or visual changes per 5 s window with ffmpeg scene detection (`select='gt(scene,0.3)'`) and flag a sharp drop after the hook. JUDGE overall polish by viewing frames.
6. **Cadence of angle, scene and story changes (lesson 7).** AUTOMATE detection of cut density over time. Map the 3–5 s, 10 s and 20–30 s targets to checks, with scene-change timestamps as a proxy. Treat true "new mini story" beats as JUDGE.
7. **Purposeful switches (lesson 8).**
   - AUTOMATE the mechanics: punch-in and zoom-out via `zoompan` or `crop`/`scale` with time expressions, and speed ramps via `setpts` and `atempo`.
   - JUDGE the shot-type choices: confirm with a vision model that close-ups land on emotional moments and wides on scale beats.
8. **Something changes every 1–3 s (lessons 9–10).**
   - AUTOMATE a scheduler in Python. Compute the gap between change events (cuts, B-roll, new text or animation, zooms) and insert a punch-in, a text pop (ASS caption) or a B-roll swap wherever the gap exceeds the target. Use about 1 s for Gen Z and about 2–3 s as the default.
   - JUDGE the audience fit. For older audiences, use a slower cap and check the result visually.
9. **Progress bar that starts fast then slows (lesson 11).** AUTOMATE.
   - Render the bar with an eased progress function, for example 50% at 10 s and slower afterwards.
   - Options: an ASS vector drawing animated with `\t` and `\clip`; PIL-generated frames overlaid with ffmpeg `overlay`; or a pre-rendered bar image with a moving `x='...t...'` expression over a track.
   - Match the demo style: rounded bar, cyan-to-purple gradient, glow, grey track, in the lower third.
10. **Visible timer ("see results in 15 seconds") (lesson 12).** AUTOMATE.
    - Generate one ASS event per second (the "00:09" style banner), or use `drawtext` with `%{eif\:...}` expansion for a countdown.
    - Style it as a yellow banner with black condensed bold caps, or a dark pill with white digits.
    - JUDGE the placement so it doesn't cover faces or the product.
11. **Testimonial progress bar (lesson 13).** AUTOMATE. Compute the start and end times of each testimonial clip and drive a segmented or filling bar from those times.
12. **VSL countdown before the reveal (lesson 14).**
    - AUTOMATE the title card and countdown, placed at (reveal time − 20 s) with a duration equal to the stated number.
    - JUDGE whether the content during the countdown actually reinforces earlier points. Have an LLM check that the VO or captions repeat the pain points.
13. **Why timers work (lesson 15).** SKIP. It is rationale only.
14. **Recap (lesson 16).** JUDGE as a final QA checklist: loops stacked, cadence met, timers or progress present, and a hook that earns a pause.
